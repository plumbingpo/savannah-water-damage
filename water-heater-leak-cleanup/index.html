Water Heater Leak Cleanup in Savannah: What to Know

A water heater holds 40 to 80 gallons, but that is not the limit of the leak. The tank is connected to your supply line, so when the bottom rusts through it refills as fast as it empties. Until someone closes the cold water valve, it keeps running. How bad it gets depends on placement, whether that is the garage, a closet in the hall or an attic sitting above finished rooms, which is the worst.

Our Savannah team extracts the water, then looks at what surrounded the heater. Usually the tank platform and the lower part of the closet walls have taken on the most water. In a garage, water reaches the wall shared with the house and anything stored on the floor. Water from an attic unit soaks through insulation, then through the ceiling. Each of those areas is metered, then dried using dehumidifiers and air movers.

Do This First

Before We Arrive

  1. Turn Off the Heat SourceFor an electric heater, switch off its breaker. For gas, turn the control knob to off. A burner or heating element must not stay on once the tank is empty.
  2. Close the Cold SupplyFind the cold water shutoff above the heater and turn it clockwise. When that valve leaks or will not move, close the main water valve to the house.
  3. Watch for Hot WaterTank water is hot enough to scald. Pets and children should stay clear, and nobody should walk into pooled water before it cools down.
  4. Empty the Tank OutsideIf you can reach the drain valve, attach a garden hose and run it outside or to a floor drain so the remaining water does not empty onto the floor.
  5. Clear the FloorGet rugs, boxes and other absorbent things up off the wet floor. Cardboard and fabric soak up water quickly and hold it against walls and concrete.
Coverage

What About Insurance?

Water damage to belongings, walls and floors from a tank that fails suddenly is often covered as a sudden and accidental loss. Usually the unit is left out of the claim, as age related wear is viewed as a maintenance matter. Because coverage usually does not extend to gradual leaks, a claim may be denied or reduced when the adjuster sees indications the tank leaked for a long time, like old stains or trails of rust. Check your policy, photograph the heater and the damage, and keep the old unit until the claim is settled.

Cost

What Drives the Price

  • Placement of the unit, as leaks from an attic or an inside closet run higher than those in a garage.
  • The length of time the tank went on refilling until someone closed the supply valve.
  • How many stored belongings got wet and need to be moved, dried or hauled away.
  • Whether flooring in adjoining rooms was reached and what type it is.

The Usual Culprits

Why This Happens

  • Fittings left slightly loose by recent repair or installation work.
  • An aging steel tank whose bottom is eaten through by corrosion and sediment.
  • Anode rods that are used up, leaving the steel inside the tank open to rust.
  • Drain pans beneath closet or attic units that rust through or clog and then spill over.
  • A missing expansion tank or a failed thermostat that lets too much pressure build inside.
If It Is Left

What the Water Damages

Stored Belongings

The first casualties are usually the furniture, boxes and keepsakes stored around the heater in a garage or closet. Mold can develop on damp contents that are not promptly dried or tossed, while cardboard boxes disintegrate.

Stand and Subfloor Damage

More water goes into the subfloor or wooden stand beneath the tank than anywhere else. That wood may rot and lose strength, and it will have to carry the weight of a new tank filled with water.

Adjacent Room Flooring

Carpet, hardwood or laminate in the hallway gets wet as water escapes below the closet door. Heat makes it worse, with quicker swelling of wood boards and carpet backing that comes apart.

Attic and Ceilings

Water from an attic unit runs down through insulation to the ceiling beneath it. Soaked insulation loses its insulating value and gets heavy enough to collapse the drywall ceiling.
